## The problem

Temple helps people focusing on blood sugar, blood pressure, and cholesterol shop for their actual health goals, not just count calories. The gap I kept finding in research: a doctor says "cut sugar," and the patient is standing in the cereal aisle with no idea what that means when every box claims to be heart-healthy. Early testing surfaced three separate breakdowns: a landing page that read as "another wellness app" instead of a real translation tool, an onboarding flow that asked for an account before it had earned any trust, and a food-scanning feature that graded people with a binary "recommended / not recommended" verdict that felt punishing rather than useful.

## Key design decisions

<StatCompare before="4.1%" after="7.8%" label="Landing page form completion, sequential A/B test" />

<Decision title={"Make the landing page say \"translates your doctor's orders,\" not \"another wellness app\""}>

A 5-second test on the early version showed almost nobody could name what Temple actually did. I rewrote the hero around the real job, translating clinical advice into a grocery list, and changed the scanner language from "Not recommended" to "Skip it" to match the tone of the product itself. Form completion nearly doubled, from 4.1% to 7.8%, in a sequential A/B test.

</Decision>

<Decision title="Ask for goals before asking for an account" image="/images/work/temple/onboarding.png" alt="Temple onboarding Goals screen with multi-select" caption="Onboarding Goals screen">

Usability testing showed 4 of 6 participants stalling at account creation because they didn't yet understand why Temple needed one. I restructured onboarding so goal selection (multi-select, not one static mode) and allergen filtering came first, with account creation pushed to the last step. Stalls dropped to zero in the next round of testing.

</Decision>

<Decision title="Replace the binary verdict with a three-tier system people would actually use" image="/images/work/temple/scanner.png" alt="Temple scanner result card with Go for it, Watch portions, Skip it verdicts" caption="Scanner result card, three-tier verdict">

The original scanner copied the "recommended / not recommended" pattern competitors use, and it read as judgment, not guidance. People said they'd stop scanning foods they knew they'd eat anyway rather than face a red flag. I designed a three-tier verdict instead, Go for it / Watch portions / Skip it, and tested it against the binary version. Real-world scanning more than doubled per shopping trip after it shipped, and it became the core interaction in the product.

</Decision>

## Reflection

The hardest part of Temple wasn't any single screen, it was knowing when a feature I believed in wasn't one I could deliver responsibly. I wanted Temple to support halal and kosher preferences alongside blood sugar, blood pressure, and cholesterol goals. But verifying a product against either standard requires certification I had no way to confirm at my scale, and guessing would mean false confidence about something tied to someone's faith. So, I removed it, and kept only the preferences I could verify. The same logic applied to the scanner: I kept the three-tier verdict over the industry-standard binary because it was the version people actually used.

Building this solo meant I had to be the check on my own instincts, not just my own advocate. The halal and kosher call is the clearest example: it felt like the right feature until the evidence said otherwise, and I let the evidence win. That's the standard I hold every decision to now, not whether I like it, but whether I can prove it.
